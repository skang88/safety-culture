## INTRODUCTION

Safety professionals do not have to resolve safety management system or culture issues in isolation from the rest of the organization. A core element of an effective safety culture is the employee involvement in the safety management system. Safety Culture. https://doi.org/10.1016/B978-0-12-814663-7.00008-X When engaged, experienced employees can be indispensable problem-solvers as they are closest to the real and potential hazards and associated risk. Employee involvement in communicating hazards and risks is critical to the safety management system, given the potential for hidden hazards and uncontrolled risk being present. A safety management system depends on the interaction between all employees including the leadership team. The primary goal of this interaction is to develop methods for identifying hazards with processes in place to reduce or control exposure to hazards and associated risk. Interaction through open and positive communication increases the potential for developing and implementing better solutions to identified safety-related issues. An assessment must be made on how to best engage employees at all levels of the organization. Increased positive relationships can potentially enhance the organization’s safety culture as employees become part of the solution and not considered disinterested or not capable of providing insights on operational issues. ## WHY SHOULD EMPLOYEES BE INVOLVED?

The leadership team provides the vision and mission of the organization along with goals and objectives believed to best guide the organization. The leadership team, with its many obligations and general issues, may overlook concerns where employees have developed a detailed knowledge of the overall steps and tasks needed to get their job done. The authors have encountered this situation when assisting organizations in developing or completing job hazard analysis. They found in various cases, what leadership thinks is being specifically done can be very different from what employees were doing. Employee involvement in the safety process becomes vital in identifying and bringing hidden hazards and risk to light. Employees are directly exposed to operational hazards as they perform their job assignments using the job’s required steps, tasks, and subtasks. While employees may know what it takes to complete a task, they may not be able to describe the specific hazards and risk inherent in the organization. From experience and “word of mouth,” employees may have developed informal ways of controlling or avoiding loss-producing events not shared with the leadership team or the safety professional. In many cases, they may be accepting uncontrolled hazards and risk as part of their job. The following basic actions can encourage employees to get involved:
* Involving employees in safety efforts.
* Inviting experienced employees to become “problem-solvers” in safety-related issues, risk assessments, accident investigations, and so on.
* Involving employees in reviewing and completing job hazard analysis.
* Encouraging employees to offer their ideas and suggestions (Managing Worker Safety and Health, 2016).

## REASONS EMPLOYEES ARE NOT INVOLVED IN THE SAFETY PROCESS

Getting employees involved in any safety process requires a step back to look at reasons why employees cannot or do not want to be involved. One reason that employees may not want to be involved is the fact that there is no formal safety process to allow involvement.

To begin understanding why employees are not participating, start with how the organizations views or perceives what constitutes “safety.” Safety in this view impedes productivity or is seen as not part of their job. Any safety activities are limited to physical items mandated by regulatory requirements and not specific to what they do. This perspective of safety limits the need for any employee involvement.

The authors have experienced, when a negative perception of safety exists, selection of personal protective equipment (PPE) is relegated to a purchasing budget and little thought given to what is purchased, how employees are fitted with equipment, and trained in its care and use. In a safety management system, before any selection, distribution, wearing, inspection, and replacement of PPE, a full assessment is to be completed to verify a hazard is present and cannot be controlled by other actions. In a strong, positive safety culture, employees would be involved in the selection to determine if the PPE meets the control needs and can be properly worn. Its effective use becomes an espoused value backed by deep-seated values. PPE Selection will be discussed in more detail in Chapter 12 Using the Job Hazard Analysis. Managers often complain that they cannot get workers to comply fully with required safety measures, whether that means wearing appropriate personal protective equipment or following safe work procedures. Managing Worker Safety and Health (2016) In completing a hazard identification, observation may show employees are not using or properly wearing assigned PPE. The immediate feedback from this observation implies employees are not actively involved or engaged in the safety process. Simple observation opens the door to multiple questions regarding all aspects of the safety process. The structure provided by the safety management system and a general assessment of hazard and risk controls may show gaps in how specific safety programs are implemented and communicated. For example, PPE is to be provided as the final line of defense of hazard control only after the steps of the Hierarchy of Controls have been assessed as advocated in the safety process.

### Caution
Remember, PPE has its place but is not the solution to all problems. There are many more hazards that need to be addressed and resolved that sometimes we tend to overlook. PPE is easy to implement and control where hazards controls are not always an easy solution. Most of the time, this is one of the areas that seems to be a default solution for many situations. Remember, PPE is only one part of a good safety management system. We would encourage you to look beyond the PPE as a solution in many cases.

### Observation 1
James had a case where an employee caught a foreign object in an eye. Even though the plant had no history of eye injuries, the leadership team immediately decided to change the entire eye protection policy and program, scrapped all currently used eye protection, and replaced them with new types of glasses. The foreign object was a piece of dust in the eye, not from any operational hazard. Employees were not involved in the incident review or assessment. No risk assessment, employee discussions, or analysis of the work environment was completed. The solution was implemented for what may not have been a safety problem. A second case involved an organization using a safety guideline where at any time an injury occurred involving a hand tool, the tool thought to be the culprit would be banned from use. When an employee using a utility knife cut his leg, immediately, without investigating to determine the possible “root cause,” all utility knives in the organization were banned from use. The banned tools or items were necessary for certain job activities and banning them created production issues. The perception of safety became negative. Using these examples, what is the perception held by employees about the safety efforts of these organizations? Each scenario resulted in actions making employee tasks harder to complete without changing how the job was to be completed. The common theme in these cases is employees were not involved in the assessment process or asked for their insights before a mandated solution was forced on them. This type of approach is authoritarian and cannot be considered a participatory organizational culture. If the response to problems is made with limited discussions and analysis, why would employees want to be involved in solving safety issues? The methods used to respond to problems provide sources of information about the strength or weakness of the safety culture and the safety process.

### Listen to Employees
An effective approach when developing any safety management system is to “just listen” to what employees have to say. As simple as this seems, it is the most direct way to learn about the current safety process viability, that is, what works and what may not be feasible under current conditions. The approach can be difficult for leadership or managers who may find it hard to let go of their traditional approach of a top-down authoritarian style. The culture of the organization may have a value system that reduces or prevents the direct involvement of employees, functioning as a “Do as we say” with no questions or feedback allowed. The leadership team may have a basic understanding of the operational aspects of the organization but have a limited understanding of the leadership concepts focusing on getting employees involved in the process.

### Lesson Learned 1
James used a simple “rule of thumb.” When interviewing employees during an accident investigation or other safety inquiries, he would try to listen 80% of the time and only talk 20% of the time. In this way, he found he could usually get to the potential root issues of a loss-producing event by listening to all the facts before he began asking questions. Refer Chapter 6, Creating the Safety Process for a discussion regarding 80/20 rule. Too often, an “Information Dump” is used by leadership on employees. An “information dump” is where, unfortunately, the most discussion time is spent by leaders letting their audience hear about how much they know as opposed to listening and becoming aware of what questions and concerns are being communicated by others. The information dump provides the leader with the good feeling of having accomplished letting others know how much they know. Nothing of value is learned or communicated as an information dump stops mutual learning and prevents employee involvement. Daniel Pink suggests listening for employee use of “We” and “They” in discussions. He gives credit for this to Robert Reich, a former U.S. Labor Secretary. If employees use “we,” it indicates they “feel they are part of something significant and meaningful.” If “they” is used, it signals that “some amount of disengagement or perhaps even alienation” may be present. (Pink, 2011)

## GETTING EMPLOYEES INVOLVED IN THE SAFETY PROCESS

In general, taking the approach of “my way or the highway” can result in hidden resistance or passive acceptance. Resistance is sabotage or ignoring the communicated mandate. Passive acceptance results in employees doing what is mandated, even when the mandate may not be correct. Either of these approaches prevents or eliminates constructive dialog about concerns or problems. As a starting point, an effort should be made to determine what level of trust exists between employees and the leadership team. Employee perception of safety may be based on experience, current or in the past, where their involvement resulted in immediate negative feedback. To overcome this perception, it is important to make an effort to mend any mistrust and miscommunication between the leadership team and employees. The leadership team can mend employee communication by showing visible, positive action by:
* Communicating the written policy stating a safe work environment is to be maintained, followed by specific leadership
actions supporting the policy.
* Conducting one-on-one discussions with employees to understand their expectations for maintaining a safe work
environment.
* Assigning employees specific safety responsibilities.
* Providing adequate time, budget, and resources for the assigned responsibilities.
* Taking employees’ suggestions and reports of safety-related issues seriously.
* Implementing a method for gathering and communicating employee safety suggestions. The method would ensure all
suggestions are promptly reviewed, implemented, or an explanation provided as to why the safety suggestion was delayed or rejected (Managing Worker Safety and Health, 2016).

## GUIDELINES FOR EMPLOYEE INVOLVEMENT

Employees who are asked to participate in the development of safety rules and procedures have a personal stake in the safety process. Employees are more apt to support ideas they helped to develop and implement (Recommended practices for safety and health programs in construction, 2016). Changes may need to be addressed as to how to best provide employees with the opportunity to get involved. Basic key elements used as a guide to improve involvement at all levels of the organization include the following:
* Communicating with employees regularly to update them on the status of workplace safety-related issues.
* Providing employees with complete access to relevant information on specific safety-related programs specific to their
job assignments.
* Familiarizing employees with methods to assess and identify hazards, prioritizing results of hazard and risk assessments, training criteria, and safety management system evaluations.
* Establishing a process to report injuries, first-aid cases, “near misses,” loss-producing events, new or developing
hazards and risks.
* Providing prompt responses to suggested employee recommendations.
* Involving employees in defining methods to identify specific hazards and assist in fixing the hazards under their direct
control.
* Involving employees in developing or revising workplace safety rules.
* Providing current, newly hired, transferred, and seasoned employee site-specific safety training.
* Involving employees in developing and presenting safety-related information at safety meetings.
* Emphasizing a team approach where employees support each other by interpersonal actions.
* Involving leadership and employee teams in conducting site inspections, safety walk-throughs, and so on.
* Providing the time and resources for employees to participate with the leadership team and other advisory or specialpurpose safety committees.
* Involving employees in safety-related decisions throughout the organizational operation.
(Recommended Practices for Safety & Health Programs in Construction, 2016)

## “JUST ASK” EMPLOYEES TO GET INVOLVED

The simplest approach is the most overlooked and the best, “Just ask the employees” if they want to become involved in the safety process. Employees then become volunteers, and their effort does not have to be mandated involvement.

### Lessons Learned 2
James asked a plant manager why employees would not get involved in the safety process. The plant manager stated that he had done everything he knew to get his employees active and could not understand why no one wanted to be involved. The question was asked, “Have you just asked them?” The plant manager’s response was “no,” he had not directly asked anyone. As it turned out, the plant was multicultural with many non-English speaking employees. The plant manager had been reluctant to ask employees for their involvement because he did not speak their language. James and the plant manager started to talk to the employees, asking employees if they would like to participate in resolving safety issues. The overwhelming response was “yes,” and involvement improved. The employees knew enough English to communicate.

## SIMPLE BEGINNINGS CAN GENERATE AN IMPACT

When participation is viewed as establishing a network, leadership can better understand that employee involvement provides a vital link to a more effective safety management system. When communications are viewed as following a network, how influence begins to spread can be assessed even with only a relatively small number of involved, active employees. As safety issues are resolved and communicated, a positive reputation and trust “meme” begins to develop as other employees see leadership is serious about employee participation and how their involvement directly benefits them.

### Safety Committees
Safety committees are the focal point for employee involvement. They have been considered as part of what constitutes a traditional safety program. An effective safety committee can readily spread a positive message for safety-related activities and efforts through an organization. Implementing a safety committee includes establishing a charter of its mandate and structure, training on the basics of holding a meeting, and core training on the safety management system to sustain its effectiveness. Safety committees are usually established without much thought as to their purpose and what they are to accomplish. How a committee is to be established, used, and managed should be given thoughtful planning before they are implemented. The problem is that when not properly implemented or managed, the potential exists for committees becoming a misguided gripe session, degrading the safety culture instead of enhancing it. When this occurs, the purpose of the safety committee is derailed from its original concept. Selecting safety committee members can be a daunting task and does not come without criticism. Problems and issues teams face include:
* The teams have no clear directions and no visible reason to exist. Little or no plan, mandate, structure, or schedule is
established.
* Leadership is too busy to participate routinely.
* Agendas are not used to guide the topics and use of committee time.
* Meetings become stale and do not provide learning opportunities.
* Discussions regarding safety efforts do not reach decisions or action planning.
* Employees are selected from personnel who are not interested or have any team experience.
* Individuals do not show up for meetings or participate in the meetings.
* The committee leader does not have the necessary leadership skills to run a successful committee.
* Meeting agendas or follow-up minutes are not being communicated promptly to the organization.
Before developing or restructuring a safety committee, review if other committees are being used in the organization. If successful meeting formats and charters exist in other parts of the organization and are effectively used, model the safety committee after those committees. For example, if a successful quality committee has been in operation, its meeting structure, charter, and administration may already be familiar to leadership and employees. The safety committee could make minor changes to its charter and not have to reinvent the wheel as to how to organize and manage itself. Group decisions benefit from the many points of view and varied experiences of the safety committee members (Managing Worker Safety and Health, 2016). A committee may not be the best and fastest way to reach a decision, but group decisions are often the best choice for getting employee buy-in on safety-related matters. If the safety committee has a good cross-section of employees drawn from throughout the organization, the diversity of the group can better communicate the committee’s efforts throughout the organization as well as increase the potential for improving the committee’s problem-solving skills. A safety committee is not the best approach to decisions involving immediately dangerous to life and health situations. In these cases, immediate corrective actions must be taken, and the committee can be used to determine why the situation developed. The committee can review the decisions made and identify gaps in the safety management system. The safety committee does not replace the responsibility and accountability of the leadership team for ensuring a safe work environment is maintained. The safety committee is an advisory team, but greatly magnifies the potential for focusing the creative energy and expertise of employees on hazards and associated risk. As it becomes embedded in the overall social network of the organization, it acts as a way to expedite information rapidly to the leadership team and employees that may otherwise be blocked for whatever reason. A safety committee can short circuit across the internal boundaries of the organization.

## ESTABLISHING THE TEAM CHARTER

A charter is recommended to define the scope and structure of a safety committee. A charter is the document providing guidance over the life of the committee and gives the reason for the establishment of the committee. In project management, a project charter or project definition (sometimes called the terms of reference) is a statement of the scope, objectives, and participants in a project. It provides a preliminary delineation of roles and responsibilities, outlines the project objectives, identifies the main stakeholders, and defines the authority of the project manager. It serves as a reference to the authority for the future of the project. Charter (2018) The charter is developed as a joint effort between the leadership team and the members of the initial safety committee. The charter is a living document and must be periodically reviewed and amended as conditions warrant. Its core purpose and structure should be kept as permanent as possible. The establishment of a safety committee is essentially the same as prescribed for the development of any project team. To develop a committee or team charter effectively, a series of questions need to be answered to assess the committee/ team’s potential duties, accountabilities, and authority. The following questions develop the basic content of the safety committee charter:
* “What is the purpose of this Safety Committee?
* What will be the authorities, accountabilities, and responsibilities of this Safety Committee?
* How will the Safety Committee measure its success?
* Who is the Safety Committee sponsor or champion?
* Who are the members? How long will each member serve on the committee?
* How will Safety Committee leadership be determined and rotated?
* What specific deadlines that the Safety Committee must maintain?
* How often is the Safety Committee expected to meet? How much time will members be allowed to participate?
* When is the best time for the Safety Committee to meet?
* Where will the Safety Committee meet?
* What is the budget for the Safety Committee?
* What resources and advisory expertise will be needed?
* How will reports and communications of the Safety Committee be communicated to leadership and employees? What
media will be used?” (Turner & Turner, 1998) Once a core charter has been developed, it is shared with the safety committee at its first meeting. The first meeting is critical as it will set the tone for all future meetings. Members are asked to review, discuss, and question the content of the charter at this first meeting and offer suggestions for changes. The final decision regarding the Charter is mutually made by the leadership team and safety committee members. The first step is the most important step in growing a team. The Team Charter specifies the purpose of the team, the boundaries of its scope and authority, and team membership. One of the major reasons that teams fail is that the original charter was too vague. This leads to “mission creep” in which teams spill into areas that were never intended, or teams become confused about how much authority they have and stumble into conflict with supervisors and other teams. (Roughton & Crutchfield, 2016) The committee must continue to show it is of value to the organization. If the value cannot be maintained, neither leadership nor employees will want to remain involved because they feel that the safety committee is not a productive use of their time. ## CHOOSING SAFETY COMMITTEE MEMBERS

### Observation 2
A method James found useful in the establishing of a safety committee was to let employees recommend other employees they felt would make good committee members. The recommended person is asked to suggest another person who in turn is asked to endorse a potential safety committee member. The method taps into the employees’ network and increases the spread of influence of the committee as the selection process message travels through the organization.

### The Safety Committee
Depending on the size of the organization, a safety committee can be established to provide better support for the safety process. This committee has also been called the Executive Committee or Joint Health and Safety Committee depending on the size and scope of the organization and the committee’s charter. The safety committee provides oversight of specific safety activities to ensure that each activity is accurately completed promptly (Managing Worker Safety and Health, 2016). For the safety committee, even though it is suggested, that the leadership team lead the committee, the authors have found the leadership role should rotate through the committee members. ### Permanent Sub-Committees
A wide array of activities can be taken on by the central committee. To reduce the time requirements and demands on the members, various tasks can be delegated to members who in turn lead and manage the specific subcommittee. These subcommittees coordinate and share their information to provide overall consistency and ensure that no gaps between the subcommittees develop. Permanent Sub-Committees could include, but are not limited to, the following:
* Publicity.
* Inspections and Walk-Throughs.
* Incident Reports.
* Job Hazard Analysis.
* Rules, Procedures, and Standards.
* Education and Training.
* Housekeeping.
* Follow-up.

### Publicity
The Publicity Sub-Committee’s main duty is to communicate and promote the safety management system. The committee establishes the style and media to be used and defines the network of communication that gets the safety message to all employees. It ensures that all employees are kept updated on all activities of the various subcommittee projects and activities. This committee is critical to ensuring that a positive and clear message is communicated about the desired goals and objectives designed to enhance the safety culture.

### Inspections and Walk-Throughs
Inspections, observations, and walk-throughs are a core feature of a safety management system. This Sub-Committee assists in coordinating the various types of surveys, reviews, and inspections of facilities and operations. It would review checklist and inspection formats to ensure that these remain current, kept up to date, consistent, and routinely scheduled. The Sub-Committee assists in training on each of the various formats used. This committee works with the Follow-up Team to ensure that hazardous conditions and safety issues identified are corrected or alternatives developed until full corrective action can be taken.

### Incident Reports
This Sub-Committee assists in developing and using the process to complete a review of loss-producing events. Depending on the complexity of the event, an ad hoc committee specific to the incident might be established to work with the leadership team. The Committee would provide a review of all reports for consistency and completeness. They will determine if the basic causes of the event were determined and effective corrective measures have been taken to the degree possible. This Sub-Committee will work closely with leadership as well as risk management, insurance, and legal advisors as applicable to ensure all liability and workers’ compensation criteria are properly followed.

### Job Hazard Analysis
The JHA Sub-Committee can be used to assist in developing and reviewing the job hazard analysis to ensure that job safety criteria are current and effective. This is a critical committee in that it can assist in ensuring an ongoing process of job hazard analysis is maintained. JHAs provide a method to identify hazards, the severity of associated risk, and whether the residual risk is consistently controlled. For more details, refer Chapter 12, “Developing the Job Hazard Analysis.”

## RULES, GUIDELINES, AND STANDARDS

This Sub-Committee assists in developing, updating, and ensuring consistency in the safety rules, guidelines, and standards necessary for the safety management system and maintaining safe operations and work environment.

### Observation 3
One of the authors was associated with an organization where every time an employee was injured, a process of recording the injury-related causal factors was used and incorporated into a “Book of Safety Rules.” This book had grown to 35 pages of mandated rules over the years, along with the many documented causes of injuries. Every time a new injury occurred, the book of rules would be reviewed to find out what “violation” may have occurred. If a rule were found in the book based on a past injury, then that rule would be applied to the current injury and disciplinary action taken against the injured employee. The ever-growing book took on a life of its own. The book became a bible of past occurrences primarily used as a disciplinary tool with employees expected to know 35 or so pages of rules.

### Education and Training
The Education and Training Sub-Committee assist in coordinating and supporting safety-related training and providing new training ideas, materials, references, and other resources. It ensures that safety training is consistently provided, kept current, and meets the current needs of the organization. It would advise on recommendations for closing gaps in training and provide oversight of the quality and delivery of safety training. This subcommittee would include organizational trainers and Human Resources personnel tasked with providing new-hire orientation and specific job training to ensure coordination of specific requirements, time, and budget.

### Housekeeping
An uncluttered, well and organized work environment is essential to the safety management system. Proper housekeeping management provides for an orderly arrangement of operations, tools, equipment, storage facilities, supplies, and waste material. The Housekeeping Sub-Committee tracts housekeeping or orderliness conditions to reduce hazardous work conditions. The Sub-Committee promotes positive behaviors, habits, and attitudes as they relate to maintaining a consistently high level of orderliness. It assesses the work environment by monitoring conditions, assisting leadership in identifying potential housekeeping hazards, and determines ways to correct the causes of substandard conditions or issues. The Sub-Committee mandate includes property protection and conditions affecting third parties or visitors to the organization.

### Follow-Up
The Follow-Up Committee provides oversight of safety-related maintenance and work order requests. This Sub-Committee requires special expertise and should consist of maintenance technicians, experienced employees, and a supervisor with authority to take corrective action. The Follow-Up Committee provides an unbiased review of each safety-related maintenance and work order created, scheduled, and completed within a designated period or remaining incomplete. The objective of this group is to assist the maintenance staff in setting risk-related priorities and ensure that the corrective actions are fully implemented. The Sub-Committee reviews each maintenance work order with the employee who submitted the work order. A risk assessment is completed to establish a priority for corrective action or moving the request to the next level of decision maker. When the maintenance or work order is logged as completed, a member of the committee reviews and discusses the corrective actions with the employee who submitted the request, to determine if the issue observed is corrected. If there is an agreement on the corrective action by the employee and the subcommittee member, the employee and the team member signs off on the corrective action.

### Ad Hoc Committees
One of the concerns expressed about safety committees is employees may not want to become involved in long-term committee membership. As projects are developed by the Safety Committee, short-term projects may appeal to various employees having different types of expertise and experience. Some employees may prefer the permanent Safety Committee where they can use their skills in administration and organizing while other individuals have an interest in a project of personal interest. Ad Hoc Committees are formed to complete specific projects and are limited to a specific time frame. These committees meet as often as warranted to complete assigned projects. The chairperson reports on project status to the safety committee as directed and provide regular updates on group activities and recommendations. The following are some sample Ad Hoc committees created to tackle short-term projects:
* Ergonomic-Related Issues.
* Potential safety Incentives.
* Vehicle Inspection Reviews.
* JHA Development Team.
* Fire protection reviews.
* Specific risk assessments.
* Modifications to the safety management system.

## BEST PRACTICES FOR WORKPLACE SAFETY COMMITTEES

A well-managed and effective Safety Committee, along with its various subcommittees, acts as a conduit for communication between leadership and employees. The primary value derived from using committees is their potential for maintaining a positive flow of safety-related information throughout the organization. Employee participation adds further value and a depth of experience useful in the administration of a safety management system as membership is drawn from across all areas of the organization.

## SUMMARY

Engaging employees at all levels of the organization is crucial for a safety culture. The leadership team provides the vision/mission of the organization with the goals and objectives believed to best guide the organization for continuous improvement. An effective approach when developing any safety management system is to “listen” to what employees have to say. This is the most direct way to learn about the effectiveness of a safety management system. The approach can be difficult for leaders who have not experienced employee involvement or cannot accept its value. The general culture of an organization may or may not value the direct involvement of employees. Determining if employees have a level of trust that the leadership team is serious about asking for their involvement in the safety process is crucial. In an evaluation of the organizational culture, an assessment of the level of trust indicates the potential success of the safety management system. When involvement is viewed from a networking perspective, leadership can better understand how employee participation provides a vital link to an effective safety management system. The positive influence of the safety management system can begin to spread even if only a relatively small number of involved employees are active. A safety committee should have a charter of its mandate and structure, training on the basics of holding a meeting, and core training on the safety management system, all of which are needed to sustain its effectiveness. A charter defines the scope and structure of the safety committee. A charter is a document that will provide guidance over the life of the committee and gives the reason for the establishment of the committee. A Safety Committee is an advisory committee, but can greatly magnify the potential for focusing the creative energy and expertise of employees on the control of hazards and associated risk. As it becomes embedded in the overall social network of the organization, it acts to expedite safety information rapidly to the leadership team and employees that may otherwise be blocked for whatever reason. A safety committee can provide a pathway to short circuit across existing internal boundaries of the organization. Subcommittees can be used for specific areas and special projects to allow for increased employee involvement. Ad Hoc Committees are formed to complete specific projects and are limited to a specific time frame. In Chapter 9, Defining Risk Perception, we will discuss how
* Understanding of risk and its control must permeate the organization if an in-depth safety culture is to be
sustained.
* The safety process must provide leadership with a full assessment and understanding of the organizational risk.
* A safety management system using only inspections and observations to identify hazards will not provide a full
appreciation of the potential for injury and damage if it does not link the results to potential operational and hazard risk.
