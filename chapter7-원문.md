## INTRODUCTION

Safety responsibilities require alternating between being a leader and being a manager. Management skills are necessary when implementing a safety process. Managers use a “reductionist” approach to break problems into their basic elements for analysis and the developing of solutions. Leadership is needed to communicate the vision and mission of the safety management system. Leaders inspire employees on what is expected of them for attaining and sustaining the safety culture. Leaders take systems or holistic approach and look for how all parts of the organization must work together. Safety professionals must have a basic understanding of leadership principles and how to apply them when organizing and communicating with leadership and employees on activities needed to maintain a safety culture. Leadership skills are not genetic or inherited and can be learned. Leadership qualities are essential for developing teams, committees, and developing a rapport with groups and individuals. An organization excels when it has both good managers and visionary leadership. It can survive using management skills, but without a clear leadership vision, overtime will lose the capacity to maintain itself. Managers can quickly become bureaucratic and lose sight of the original mission and vision while leaders will always keep the focus on the process. ## LEADERSHIP DEFINED

Leadership has many definitions and perceptions. Individuals can be developed into better leaders by following leadership principles and theories. “Leadership is one of those concepts that are very hard to define. More than four decades ago, Stogdill affirmed that “there are almost as many different definitions of leadership as there are persons who have attempted to define the concept” (Stogdill, 1974). Bennis estimated, at the end of the last century, at least 650 definitions of leadership in the literature (Bennis & Townsend, 1995) An old cliche states, “leadership is something you know when you see it.” The word, “leader,” immediately conveys the image of a strong-minded person who steps to the front of a group, begins to plainly and forcefully communicate what, and how the group is going to accomplish its mission. The individual then leads the way to a shared vision. This leader inspires the group to exceed expectations and achieve a vision even when others do not believe it is attainable. Leaders maintain a shared vision as to what can be accomplished and the direction to be followed despite setbacks and obstacles. Think about individuals recognized as leaders in business, military, education, religion, politics, and so on. What are their attributes? Why are they viewed as leaders? The visible presence and behavior of a leader help to develop the trust needed for the development of a safety management system. A personal leadership goal should be to increase knowledge on how to best use assigned authority and responsibilities. Actions are directed at how to best advise the leadership team on solutions for weaving the safety management system into the organizational culture. The role of the safety professional requires a wide range of expertise in many technical and management disciplines. For example, the safety professional must understand how to complete a job hazard analysis (JHA), train designated employees on how to complete the JHA, ensure quality control of the JHA process, and communicate the importance of using the JHA. ### Observation
James has worked in different organizational environments including multiplant operations created from mergers and acquisitions. One lesson he learned was to listen and observe all aspects of an organization before suggesting changes. He learned to understand better the various cultures a merger brought together as each organization had its traditions, industry languages, and approaches to managing. Given the mandates and general confusion that develops during mergers as new structures are implemented, James used a simple method to get initial information about new leadership priorities. He would call the operation managers and then wait to see who returned his call. Over time, he found, the leaders who returned his phone call immediately or in a reasonable time frame were more adaptable in accepting the new organizational structure and environment. These leaders understood a new organizational culture had to be developed and were not resisting change and became team players open to suggestions. The values, goals, objectives, business strategies, and beliefs of the organization provide indicators of its leadership style and workplace environment. These indicators send a message to the organization on what is considered important. Based on this, the leadership team sends a strong message when they:
* Are visible and active in periodic safety-related meetings such as preshift reviews, weekly/monthly meetings, and faceto-face discussions.
* Maintain open two-way communication with all employees and reduce or eliminate barriers to reporting safety issues,
hazards, and associated risk.
* Provide quick correction of loss-producing activities and conditions.
* Routinely place safety management system information on meeting agendas.
* Ensure that all safety criteria are fully incorporated into orientation and other related training.
* Track the status of the organization’s safety performance, both pre- and postloss data, with emphasis on the former.
* Take all concerns regarding safety issues, hazards, and associated risk seriously.
The inverse of the previous list creates a strong negative message to the organization when they are not in place or used. In a decentralized organizational structure, one location may have a stronger safety management system and safety culture than similar operations. A location may differ because it has a toxic style of leadership that does not allow an in-depth safety culture. One indicator might be a leadership style placing its main emphasis on just basic efforts on minimal regulatory compliance. The leadership team should be using a balance of both lagging and leading indicators regards the safety management system. ### Lesson Learned
James was with a major corporation and promoted after a few years to a supervisory role. He was told during supervision training orientation to follow the company philosophy, “if an employee cannot do their assigned job and keep up with the production line, “just fire them and hire somebody new.” For several years, he played the game and became a “good supervisor” by the company standard. A problem developed, as he never had a stable workforce because of how he was taught to manage his employees. As he moved on to another production line, the line employees’ reaction was “we don’t want to work for you,” and he continued to experience personnel turnover. He felt he was successful as a supervisor but not as a leader. The problem remained of dealing with gaining the trust and respect of his employees. He had to change his reputation of “If you do not do your job, you will be fired by James.” He began to build a stable workforce by conducting face-to-face discussions with each of his employees, to understand their needs and concerns. By conducting such discussions one-on-one, with small groups and in team meetings, he was able to communicate his new expectations. What he wanted was for anyone to come to work and just do their job to the expected quality. He changed his personal “network” from being one-way and negative,“my way or the highway! to a positive two-way communication that provided insight on obstacles to getting the job done correctly. Employees found his new leadership style had changed and he meant what he said and stood by his decisions. Once his employees understood he was fair and honest and could be approached with ideas and concerns, employee turnover became stable as employees wanted to work with him. His willingness to change by incorporating leadership principles transformed him from being a negative unapproachable supervisor to a trusted advisor. The case study reflects the following insights:
* “Leaders of integrity adhere to the values that are part of their identity and set a standard for their followers to emulate.
Identity is one’s self-concept, how one defines him or herself. Leaders who are effective with followers identify with the role and expectations of a leader; they will take responsibilities typical of a leader and perform the actions of a leader. Leaders who are unsure of themselves may not have a strong idea of their identity” (Leadership development, field manual, 6-22, 2015).

## ESTABLISHING ORGANIZATIONAL SAFETY PRIORITIES

Organizational leaders and managers may only have a general basic understanding of the value of safety. Their understanding is reflected in meeting agenda items and the scope and time allowed for safety concerns, activities, risk assessments, and discussions on safety management system issues. The leadership team may not understand the importance of concepts of hazard, associated risk, and control, and the administration of the safety management system. The safety professional’s role is to provide the expertise and leadership qualities needed to improve their perception and knowledge. The following sections offer ideas for better advising the leadership team.

### Management by Walking Around
As discussed in a previous chapter, “Management By Walking Around” (MBWA) was introduced by Peters & Waterman in their classic book “In Search of Excellence.” This technique, if used properly, suggests the leadership team should routinely and consistently be visible walking around their operation, observing, conducting one-on-one discussions with their employees to feel and tone of their operation. Good leadership makes itself visible to not just direct report staff or employees but to all levels of the organization. Peters and Waterman noted examples of where management had lost touch with the organization, an issue that still exists in many organizations (Peters & Waterman, 2004). MBWA assist in communication network development as leaders gain insight into the concerns of employees and the issues dealt with each day. Gaps in the network can be identified from these discussions. Many times, in the authors’ experience, when walking through a plant or operation, areas were noted that had never been visited by a member of the leadership team. This was especially true for multishift operations where leadership was only on site during the first shift, and limited leadership was on other shifts. As discussed in Chapter 3, Analyzing and Using Your Network, influence can spread through to three degrees of contact before becoming diluted. MBWA should be encouraged and implemented with care if the organization has not had prior leadership involvement. If the “walk-around” can result in negative impact from how the leaders conduct themselves, not following the safety rules, berating an employee, or in general mismanaged the experience, the influence of leadership is diminished. The authors have worked with leadership that excelled in being visible. Nathan visited a carpet manufacturer for insurance underwriters. Part of the questions required asking about senior management involvement in operations. His plant guide immediately told how the CEO/owner routinely toured the extensive facilities and how on one occasion came across an employee sweeping an area with a broom. The CEO asked the employee why he was not using the vacuum cleaner he had been assigned. The employee told him, it had broken and a new one had not been ordered. The CEO told him to wait for him to return, left the employee and returned with a brand-new vacuum cleaner he had gone out and purchased. Word spread about the incident and managers and supervision quickly made sure all the employees had the proper functioning equipment. Leadership is about communication and actions, and the MBWA provides visibility and a way to directly communicate with employees at their worksite.

### Open Door Policy
An open door policy is essential, as it provides a way to short circuit and bypasses normal communication channels as needed. An “open door” policy, as implied, has the leader available to employees by keeping the office door open as much as possible. One area for consideration is that the open door is not to bypass any normal chain of command or authority. It offers a “relief valve” allowing anyone who wishes to approach a leader for insights, advice, and information. A problem may exist, as being accessible means walking a fine line between encouraging employees to communicate without interfering with the normal relationship with other supervisors or managers. Care must be taken by the safety professional to ensure that there is coordination with other managers/leaders, so no conflict develops as to authority and responsibility. The open door may be a formal requirement of an organization asking leaders to maintain certain times for visits by employees with concerns or questions. It may take the form of being available “online” if computer communications are used. The open door is part of the ongoing process of expanding a network of contacts within the organization. ## DEFINING ROLES, RESPONSIBILITY, DELEGATION, AUTHORITY, AND ACCOUNTABILITY

As a business grows in complexity and the number of employees and operations increase, having one individual responsible for all the details of a safety management system may become burdensome to perform. For a system to reach its potential, it is essential that roles, responsibilities, delegation, and authority for safety-related assignments are clearly defined and coupled with an accountability structure. This is accomplished by designing safety activities that are aligned with organizational goals and objectives. A safety management system structure is more efficient and practical if assignments of responsibility are delegated to several other members of the leadership team. A manager alone cannot perform all the tasks assigned to him. To meet the targets, the manager should delegate authority. A Delegation of Authority means the division of authority and powers downwards to the subordinate. Delegation is about entrusting someone else to do parts of your job. A Delegation of Authority can be defined as subdivision and sub-allocation of powers to the subordinates to achieve effective results. Delegation of Authority (2018)

## REVIEW THE ORGANIZATION FOR SAFETY-RELATED RESPONSIBILITIES

The depth, scope, and variety of safety-related responsibilities can be extensive if a safety culture is to be achieved. Safetyrelated responsibilities need to go beyond traditional injury control and regulatory compliance. Areas of responsibility can include but not be limited to:
* Policy and manual development; status reports to leadership.
* Documentation and curation of the safety management system criteria, audits, and corrective actions.
* Operational procedures, including occupational safety, health, environmental; fleet and vehicle operations, contractor
and visitor control, security, product liability, injury, and liability insurance claims and administration.
* Risk and hazard control, procurement of equipment, tools, materials, chemicals, ergonomics, design reviews, job hazard
analysis.
* Education, training and orientation programs, communications.
* Emergency and crisis preparedness, incident investigation and analysis (Occupational Health & Safety Management
Systems, 2012). A review of the organizational structure is first completed to determine specific tasks leadership and employees will be assigned and how these roles can best contribute. The review identifies the key roles deemed vital to the safety culture. The organizational analysis considers both internal and external communication and contact networks. The organizational structure is used to understand how each position can best support the safety management system. Defining and integrating accountabilities and responsibilities into the organization begins with developing a list of job titles and descriptions of individuals who can play an essential role in the safety process. The involvement of these positions provides visible evidence on how the leadership permits and encourages activities for a sustained positive safety culture. The identified individuals, in turn, become a resource for developing additional job descriptions for individuals designated to be closely associated and routinely involved in the safety process. All leadership and employees should have assigned well-defined activities and responsibilities within the safety management system. Some job titles will have more activities and responsibilities. Assignments can range from completing operational walk-throughs, data gathering, and analysis, safety-related presentations, safety committee involvement, loss-producing events, risk assessment, and so on.

## DEVELOPING AND IMPLEMENTING JOB DESCRIPTIONS

Individual job descriptions describe the most important characteristics and responsibilities of a job title (A reference for developing a basic occupational safety and health program for small businesses, 2008). A job description describes the requirements, skill requirements, general duties, activities, responsibilities, and authority, if any, for a specific position. An area of weakness in organizations is not having the assignment of safety management system responsibilities placed directly into written job descriptions. Job descriptions define the involvement in the safety management system and can be limited. Many job descriptions are restricted to statements about maintaining OSHA compliance or other regulations. Specific safety management system duties, responsibilities, and authorities should be formalized within one’s job description. The Human Resources (HR) department is tasked with maintaining job descriptions for an organization and may already have a formal process. The current HR process should be understood before requesting changes to job descriptions. The safety professional may not have the authority to make changes to job requirements. Any change requires building a strong case as well as a close, trusting relationship with the HR department. Coordination and rapport with HR personnel can provide the opportunity to modify existing job descriptions by adding specific safety management duties, responsibilities, and authorities. Well-written job descriptions potentially enhance communication and coordination among various job positions. Job descriptions help in:
* Removing any doubt about the lines of responsibilities and authority for each job position.
* Determining that all responsibilities have been accounted for in the organization.
* Identifying how new tasks and responsibilities should be assigned.
* Developing job performance objectives and establish performance measurements.
* Improving and enhancing communication throughout the organization.
* Determining whether all responsibilities have been accounted for within the organization and whether new tasks and
responsibilities should be assigned.
* Developing job performance objectives and establishing performance measurements.
(Managing worker safety and health, 2016; Managing worker safety and health, sample assignment of safety and health responsibilities, 2018)

### Writing the Basic Job Description
Begin by writing a short “General Statement” defining specific job safety responsibilities. Describe one’s authority and the job resources needed to reach stated goals and objectives. Refer (Appendix 7.1, for a sample safety responsibilities worksheet that is useful as a starting point to document safety expectations. The worksheet is a living document and must be reviewed regularly and modified to keep up with changes in safety activities.

### Gaining Agreement on Goals and Objectives
All entities of the organization must work together, with every employee in the organization having a role in sustaining the safety management process. A connection should exist between how a job position is to be held accountable for the desired safety management system results. The more involvement of employees and leadership in discussing the overall planning for change increases the buy-in before the goals and objectives are accepted and implemented. For example, if a supervisor has little control over maintenance, hiring practice, training, budget, time, quality, and so on, then the supervisor has little control or input over the elements creating the potential for a loss-producing event. If a supervisor is held accountable for injuries that occur, the supervisor is being held accountable for human error and latent management decisions that may not be under their control (Concepts and principles, human performance improvement handbook, 2009). A better approach is to have clearly defined responsibilities based on “preloss” activities under the direct control of supervisors and employees. Accountability definitions using safety goals, strictly based on injury incident rates and loss ratios do not factor in employee experience levels, employee turnover, hours worked, production problems or deficiencies, and other conditions not under the control of a leadership team. Accountability becomes a valid leadership tool by focusing on activities within the control of specific roles or job descriptions.

### Defining Clear Goals and Assign Responsibilities
Goals and objectives are established annually for personal performance and should be aligned with the organizational goals and objectives. Consequentially, annual personal performance goals and objectives should be linked to the individual’s job description. When goals and objectives are unclear, gaps can occur in what is intended to be accomplished by the job position. Vague objectives limit how to determine if performance is lacking. The “SMART” concepts can be used for developing goals and objectives as discussed in Chapter 4, Establishing the Direction for the Safety Culture. By using the SMART concept, assignments of responsibility can be reviewed to ensure they explicitly specify the overall intention, as well as the goals and objectives are feasible under the operational environment and realistically attainable.

### Writing Job Position Goals and Objectives
Documenting goals and objectives in writing clarifies the meaning and intent of what is to be accomplished. Goals and objectives are not being set in stone, and as operational conditions under which they were developed change, they will need to be modified. An ongoing dialog with leadership and employees is essential for adjustments as warranted. Once goals and objectives are established, and in writing, questions may develop about the document and its specific expectations. Questions may develop even if extensive efforts have been made to include as many participants as feasible in their construction. The written document acts as a guide to answer questions and where necessary be used to correct areas not clearly understood. The following examples can be used to develop a process for developing appropriate goals and objectives:
* Holding a daily preshift review with all the employees at a specified time to communicate how the process will work and
get feedback on what is needed.
* Meeting individually, face-to-face with each employee and discuss safety concerns, issues, and needs.
* Conducting weekly and monthly meetings to keep employees up to date on the progress of the safety process.
* Tracking and communicating the elimination of hazards identified through inspections, incident investigations, and
weekly planned inspections.
* Reviewing the equipment/machine-specific checklist with employees and assess how it is being used to develop goals
and objectives.

### Nonsupervisory Employees
When developing safety responsibilities for nonsupervisory employees, these should include but not be limited to work rules and work practices. A short statement about the employee’s responsibility to report hazards, understand and follow the rules and work practices as applicable should be included. Safety responsibilities might include:
* Attending the daily preshift meeting at its specified time.
* Participating in the weekly or monthly meeting as appropriate.
* Assisting in reporting and eliminating identified hazards.
* Reporting hazards and associated risk when observed.
* Assisting in the investigation of loss-producing events and weekly safety inspections.
* Assisting in job hazard analysis.
* Using a “Risk Guidance Card” when conditions or hazardous conditions are identified. Refer Chapter 9, “Defining Risk
Perception,” for a detail discussion regarding the use of the “Risk Guidance Card.” (Managing worker safety and health, 2016)

## REVIEW ASSIGNED ACTIVITIES REGULARLY

The possibility can exist for a manager or supervisor meeting the written objectives, but their area of responsibility continues to have risk-related issues, near misses or no significant improvement in hazardous conditions. In this case, the underlying obstacles to improvements have not been identified or properly addressed. An in-depth review is needed of the area to determine if the goals and objectives can be achieved. The assessment would include a full review of all operational aspects of the area and interviews with key employees. The assessment should attempt to ensure that the safety criteria matches the actual operational conditions.

Delegation is a critical leadership method and involves more than just “passing the buck” to subordinates, telling them to “do what you need to do.” Leadership is still responsible for the results and cannot blame the subordinate if things go wrong. Leadership must be involved in periodic reports, performance reviews, and face-to-face discussions to assess the status of what has been delegated. Network analysis can be used to determine where and how responsibilities are shifted by assigned accountabilities and authorities. The network review can identify the location of assigned responsibilities and their impact on other personnel within the network as activities cascade through an organization. * Accountability means giving explanations for any variance in the actual performance from the expectations set.
Accountability cannot be delegated. For example, if ‘A’ is given a task with sufficient authority, and ‘A’ delegates this task to B and asks them to ensure that the task is done well, responsibility rest with ‘B,’ but accountability still rest with ‘A.’ The top-level management is most accountable. Accountability, in short, means being answerable for the result. Accountability cannot be escaped. It arises from responsibility (Accountability, 2017).
* Often, the word responsibility is used in conjunction with the word accountability. When hearing the word accountability, many people immediately equate it with responsibility and see the two as being the same (The
Performance-Based Management Handbook, Volume 3, Establishing Accountability for Performance, 2017).
* Assignment of Duties. The delegator first tries to define the task and duties to the subordinate. The task also must define
the result expected from the subordinates. Clarity of duty as expected has to be the first step in the delegation (Delegation of Authority, 2018).
* Granting of authority. Subdivision of authority takes place when a supervisor divides and shares authority with the
subordinate. It is for this reason every subordinate should be given enough independence to carry out the task as assigned. The managers at all levels delegate authority and power which is attached to their job positions. The subdivision of powers is significant to obtain effective results (Delegation of Authority, 2018).
* Creating Responsibility and Accountability. The delegation process does not end once powers are granted to the subordinates. They at the same time have to be obligatory toward the duties assigned to them. Responsibility is fundamental
as it gives power to authority. At the same time, responsibility is absolute and cannot be shifted. Accountability, on the other hand, is the obligation of the individual to carry out specific duties as per the standards of performance. Authority is delegated, responsibility is created, and accountability is imposed. Accountability arises out of responsibility and responsibility arises out of authority.
* Every manager, that is, the delegator, has to follow a system to finish up the delegation process. Equally important is the
delegate’s role which means the responsibility and accountability are attached to the authority (Delegation of Authority, 2018).

## ASSIGNING SPECIFIC RESPONSIBILITIES

The safety management system provides the defined elements on which responsibilities, authorities, and accountabilities can be established. The safety management system guides the organization in reducing errors in combination with hazards and associated risk. Lossproducing events are triggered by an initiating action which may or may not be due to direct human error(s). Errors are not necessarily the result of lower level employees physically doing something creating the event but can stem from “latent” errors built into the organization (Concepts and principles, human performance improvement handbook, 2009). The types of leadership skills necessary to support the safety management system are defined by the assigned roles and responsibilities. In turn, these roles/responsibilities define how hazards and risk are to be identified and controlled. The hazards/risk further refine and direct the safety management system. The following are examples of assigned safety roles for each level of the organization:

### Leadership Team
The senior leadership team, including safety leadership, establishes and provides the guidance, budget, and resources for carrying out the organization’s safety policies, goals, and objectives of the safety management system. As part of the leadership team, the safety leader has the responsibility for advising senior leadership on current safety management system administration, hazard and risk control, and the status of goals and objectives. To properly advise senior leadership, the safety professional should understand and use existing organizational styles of communications, presentations, and general leadership objectives. The leadership team establishes and provides the budget and resources for the safety management system. The safety professional has the responsibility for managing assigned resources and communicating valid concerns when additional resources are needed. The safety professional should understand how the organization determines resource allocations. Refer Appendix 7.2 Sample Responsibilities for The Leadership Team.

### Managers and Supervisors
Managers and supervisors are tasked with carrying out the assigned senior leadership team’s goals and objectives for maintaining safe working conditions in their respective assigned areas of responsibility. This level provides the leadership, budget, and resources for carrying out the organization’s safety policy, goals, and objectives. Managers have multiple supervisors reporting to them and provide the link between senior leadership and supervisors. Refer Appendix 7.3 for some sample responsibilities for Plant/Site Superintendents/ Managers.

### Supervisors
Supervisors are the direct link between the managers and the employees who carry out activities for products and services. Supervisors must ensure safe conditions are maintained in their specific area of responsibilities. Refer Appendix 7.4 for some sample responsibilities for supervisors.

### Employees
Employees exposed to operational hazards and associated risk have the potential for a loss-producing event that can be triggered by an initiating event. In simple terms, employee, have the responsibility for safety effort at a workplace by reporting hazards identified and offering suggestion on how to correct the identified hazard. Refer Appendix 7.5 for a sample list of task responsibilities employees. According to Senders and Moray. Human Error: Cause, Prediction, and Reduction, 1991, p. 20. (Concepts and principles, human performance improvement handbook, 2009), they define, “The initiating action is an action by an individual, either correct, in error, or in violation, that results in a facility event.”

## ESTABLISH CONSEQUENCES FOR PERFORMANCE

Consequences of performance can enhance or degrade the safety culture depending on how consequences are established and implemented. If consequences are perceived as negative, then the overall culture of the organization will perceive safety efforts from a negative point of view. A balance of both positive and negative consequences is essential with an emphasis on positive feedback. Newly assigned responsibilities require employees to go through a learning process to develop new skills and new behavior patterns and habits. A period of learning with minimal or no consequences for inadequate performance must be established for any new or modifies system. Extra personal coaching, immediate corrective action without penalty, and enhanced discussions can be used to show the “WHY” and “HOW” things should be done. Time, budget, and understanding the situation are needed to ensure a level of quality performance in safety-related activities. The goal of any accountability system should be to determine the consequences, acceptance, and responsibility for actions. Leadership and employees must understand the positive and negative consequences for performance. Consequences can reinforce and encourage the changing of old habits as well as the importance of meeting agreed-upon goals and objectives. Consequences need to be appropriate to the situation and determined by reviewing methods used to assess “failures to perform” against desired standards. Overreacting, either positively or negatively, can send the wrong message throughout an organization. An example of overreacting to a performance problem is the disciplining of a supervisor for not properly completing or conducting an incident investigation. Valuable future information and communications is lost by use of discipline without determining the supervisor’s skills or knowledge. A lack of understanding how the process works by the supervisor could be the reason for any poor initial performance. Discipline without understanding sends the message to the organization that in order to avoid “grief” from leadership, hide loss-producing incidents whenever possible. In turn, closing communications prevents an understanding of potential hazards, risk, safety issues, and scope of future loss. An essential leadership element is to maintain trust and keep communications open and moving through the organization as rapidly as possible. For further insight on this concern, see Mager and Pipe’s classic book, Analyzing Performance Problems (Mager & Pipe, 1983).

## SUMMARY

When the word “leader” is heard, it immediately conveys an image of an individual who can literally step to the front of a group, clearly communicate what he or she desires the group to do, inspires the group to do more than what is normally expected, and get the group to achieve what others did not think was possible. The leadership team, by its visible presence and behavior, sets the tone for driving the development of the safety management system. A safety manager’s leadership goal should be to increase personal influence by following good leadership principles. The safety manager is then in a better position to advise the leadership team on actions necessary for improving the safety management system. The safety management system and safety culture are influenced and shaped by how the organization is managed, organized, and led by the leadership team. A central structure can have an organizational culture that readily reinforces the safety culture. A well-led and managed participatory safety management system is the model for an in-depth safety culture. Achieving safety excellence begins with leadership commitment. Most organizational leaders have a general understanding of the value of safety. However, an organization must set priorities for all its operational areas for budget and resources and be successful. Safety management system Leadership must be visible and remain in contact with employees at all levels of an organization. A senior leadership team responsibility is the implementation of a safety management system. For a safety management system to reach all levels and areas of the organization, the leadership team should actively involve all employees of the organization in the process. It is important for safety-related assignments, roles, responsibility, delegation, and authority to be clearly defined and coupled with an accountability structure. Once the organizational structure and communication flows are understood, specific leadership and employee tasks can be developed. The safety management system mission and its objectives can be assigned as to what these roles can contribute. The review identifies the key roles most vital to the maintaining of a safety culture. Delegation is more than just “passing the buck” to a subordinate, telling them to “do what they need to do.” Leadership, by definition, is responsible for the final results and cannot blame the subordinate when things go wrong. The leadership triad of responsibility, authority, and accountability cannot be separated and must be considered as interrelated and together. Consequences of performance can enhance or degrade the safety culture depending on how consequences are established. If consequences are primarily regarded by employees as negative, then the overall culture of the organization will perceive safety in a negative point of view.
