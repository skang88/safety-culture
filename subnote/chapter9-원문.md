## RISK MANAGEMENT CONCEPTS
Risk management concepts have been advocated and used for many years in high hazard industries. Most safety programs are still designed around regulatory compliance and continue using primarily post-loss data with only limited use of basic risk principles. Risk management involves the systematic application of an array of techniques used to identify hazards within organizations combined with an assessment of inherent risk. An open line of two-way communication is essential to effectively share information through an organization about its scope of risk. The intent of a risk management process should be clearly defined and communicated accurately and objectively to the leadership team and employees. Risk management elements should be incorporated into each component of the organization for a safety system to be effective and for an understanding of the current level of any potential risk.

## RISK
Risk, as defined in ANSI/AIHA/ASSE Z10, is “an estimate of the combination of the likelihood of an occurrence of a hazardous event or exposure(s), and the severity of injury or illness that may be caused by the event or exposures. Risk analysis concepts provide a method of establishing and prioritizing events” (Occupational Health & Safety Management Systems, 2012). A shift in the perception of safety is necessary to redirect emphasis to include risk data and not rely strictly on loss information. When perception is changed, risk management becomes the foundation for the safety system and a better potential for minimizing loss-producing events can be developed (Mishap Prevention Program, 2011).

## WHY ARE UNNECESSARY RISK TAKEN?
Risks are intangible and hidden in the fabric of the work environment. When dealing with any hazard-related condition and its associated risk, humans seem to have a built-in affinity to view the current environment based on past experiences or history. A hazardous situation may be present, but similar situations in the past did not result in problems. Actions are then taken based on what previous consequences and not on the existing potential for a loss-producing event. A safety management system must include processes to identify hazards and their associated risk. The structure of such process would include a continuous proactive identification of existing and new potential risks with techniques to assess the possible severity of any loss-producing event. Many hazards can, over time, be considered routine and become accepted as a permanent part of an organization and controlled as such. The organizational culture may celebrate or embrace taking the risk as part of doing business, be part of the tradition as a way of life, or a way of proving one’s self. We may observe potential risks but to remain considered part of the group, we follow the behavior of others and continue to take the risk knowing the possibility of injury is present. For example, if an employee puts their hand into a piece of running equipment to unjam it and is not injured, the behavior may be continued and be mimicked over time. The “meme” activated is “getting the job done” and takes precedence over following a safe course of action that may slow a process down. The risk-taking behavior meme spreads through the immediate work environment and influences other employees. Refer Chapter 3, “Analyzing and Using Your Network” on to how to define “meme” and risk perception. People’s views on risks and their value judgments are not static, but change according to circumstances. (Mullai, 2006) Once a risk is taken, and nothing bad happens, the human tendency is to do it again without considering the consequences. The potential for an incident has been masked by no immediate incident and reinforced over time if nothing happens. At some point in time, the various actions of at-risk behavior cross with the uncontrolled hazard(s) resulting in injury, damage, or another loss-producing event.

## RISK ASSESSMENT TOOL DEFINED
The risk assessment tool is used by employees to conduct a self-assessment before performing a task that may have inherent hazards. The risk assessment tool is in the form of a small card carried by all employees. This tool will be discussed in detail later in this chapter. The intent is to have all employees, including the leadership team, to be able to
* Recognize hazards specific to their work environment.
* Analyze the potential associated risk, its potential severity, and the exposure to the hazard.
* Develop with leadership, a mutually agreed-upon risk control for the hazard.
The risk assessment tool may require a shift in the perception held by leadership on their role in the organization. This tool provides a mechanism to allow employees to raise issues without fear and with an understanding that disagreement is ok. If people only communicate good news, or if everyone pretends to agree, much less knowledge is generated and fewer discoveries were made. To be most effective, [a] challenge process must include people with different perspectives, kinds of knowledge, and expertise. (Koch, 2007) If no agreement can be reached between the employee and the immediate supervisor, the discussion is taken to the next or a higher level. The overall intent is to ensure that communication of concerns about risk is allowed to move through the organization as necessary without obstruction or delay. The main format that defines the tool includes:
* A simple risk matrix that is easy for all employees to understand.
* A process that can be used at a moment’s notice or as a routine part of task assessment.
* Consistent use by all levels of the organization.
* Being able to assess a broad range of risks.
When undertaking a risk assessment, the consequence or ‘how bad’ the risk being assessed is must be measured. In this context, consequence is defined as the outcome or the potential outcome of an event. There may be more than one consequence of a single event. (A risk matrix for risk managers, 2008) When assessing the likelihood, it is important to take into consideration the controls already in place. The likelihood score is a reflection of how likely it is that the adverse consequence described will occur. (A risk matrix for risk managers, 2008) Likelihood can be scored by considering: frequency (how many times will the adverse consequence being assessed be realized?) Or probability (what is the chance the adverse consequence will occur in a given reference period?). (A risk matrix for risk managers, 2008)

## CHANGING PERCEPTIONS
As discussed previously, The shift from a Loss-based process using Total Case Incident Rates (TCIR) or other postloss ratios to a Risk-based process has been long advocated. TCIR can be a valuable measure but cannot be the only factor used to assess organizational safety performance. To develop a basic measuring system, the elements of the Activity-Based Safety System as discussed in Chapter 11 can be used as a good starting point. “A risk is acceptable when it is acceptable to the general public” (Fewtrell & Bartram, 2001). What is acceptable in the general public may not be acceptable in an organization and may be detrimental to its safety culture. Legal systems and case law have established rules and regulations organizations must follow addressing their liabilities concerning uncontrolled hazards and risks. For example, employees may, on their own time, take extreme risk, such as skydiving, rock climbing, drive a vehicle on weekends without using a seat belt, and so on. The concern is this mind-set of risk acceptance may get into the workplace unless a mechanism is in place to prevent unacceptable risk.

## MEETING AND GETTING TO SUSTAINABILITY
Individuals, perceive risk differently based on past experiences, and an objective formal tool is needed instead of a subjective personal opinion which may not properly analyze a risk severity level. A personal risk assessment tool reduces the gap between different perceptions of risk held by individuals. The value of the risk assessment tool must be communicated to the leadership team who can support and champion its use. The process starts with getting immediate leadership to see the value of the tool through demonstration and going through the risk perception process. By using the tool to evaluate existing known hazards for inherent associated risk, the value of the tool is demonstrated. As leadership begins to see its value and stresses its usefulness, that value spreads through their network in multiple directions into the organization. The next phase of introducing the tool begins with small group meetings where the value and ease of use are demonstrated. As buy-in from these initial core groups is achieved, the word should spread informally and formally through the organization.

## OBSERVATION—CAUTIONARY NOTES
In one organization, James found when the term “rollout” for a project was used, it had developed a negative connotation with most employees. ## HAZARD AND RISK DEFINED
Hazard is defined as “A condition, set of circumstances, or inherent property that can cause injury, illness, or death” (Occupational Health & Safety Management Systems, 2012). Also, the potential to cause damage or create a loss-producing event of any type must be considered. The risk is “an estimate of the combination of the likelihood of an occurrence of a hazardous event or exposure(s) and the severity of injury or illness that may be caused by the event or exposures” (Occupational Health & Safety Management Systems, 2012). Again, we expand this to include damage or any other type of loss. Risk Assessment is the “process used to evaluate the level of risk associated with hazards and systems issues.”

## RISK ACCEPTANCE
Risk management is a logical process that weighs the potential cost of losses against the possible benefits of various levels of control ranging from no control implemented to instituting. But we must learn to balance the possible negative consequences of risk against the potential benefits. (Mishap prevention program, 2011) The decision to accept risk should not be made on strictly “return on investment” criteria. It should consider the impact on the community, consumers, environment, legal duties, and many other “costs” considerations. Risk identified should outline the assessment and logic of how and why risk is considered acceptable and how the risk level does not produce harm to anyone.

The perception of risk may be different from person to person. Without a baseline method to assess risk, risk acceptance, or tolerance may challenge your assessment and overall concerns. By understanding risk tolerance and personal and organizational factors, better strategies can be developed that cut through perceptions about the acceptance of risk. We routinely see actors jumping or falling from high buildings and slamming into the pavement, stunned but immediately able to get up and continue the chase. ## THE RISK GUIDANCE CARD, A POTENTIAL SOLUTION
If a safety management system is in place, a hazard control program should have already been implemented and have addressed general safety-related criteria. To enhance the safety process, a risk guidance card, a small card distributed to all employees, helps to address any ongoing perception employees believe directly affects them as they perform their assigned tasks. This card is to be integrated into the safety process. The intent of the risk guidance card is to allow employees to assess levels of perceived risk, and if believed by the employee to be unacceptable, begin a dialog with the appropriate leadership level on whether to accept or reject the risk and what controls are to be implemented. The risk guidance card can be used to address ongoing employee perceptions or concerns about job hazards. The risk guidance card can be used with a work team and employees as part of a prejob assessment. The card provides a “Go/No Go” decision method as the employee(s) performs assigned tasks (Roughton & Crutchfield, 2013). The risk guidance card is used until the perception is mutually consistent with the concerned employee(s) and leadership team. A risk assessment matrix is part of the risk guidance card. This matrix helps the employee to evaluate their perception of the potential risk, severity, and the frequency of exposure to a hazard. A brief list of guidelines and questions are placed on the card to guide the assessment and discussions. An employee or a work team can use the risk guidance card as part of a “prejob assessment” (Human performance tools for individuals, work teams, and management, 2009) and when concerns are expressed that additional or existing hazard controls are needed. A risk assessment matrix is a useful tool to identify the level of risk and the levels of management approval required for any Risk Management Plan. There are various forms of this matrix, but they all have a common objective to define the potential consequences or severity of the hazard versus the probability or likelihood of the hazard. (Safety management system toolkit, 2007) Assessing risk must be used in full knowledge that risk perceptions can differ. The intent behind the use of a risk guidance card is to allow employees to assess levels of risk and begin the process of determining the level of risk severity that may be present. The objective is to reduce the probability that a high risk is accepted without discussion with immediate leadership. The lowest level risk (1) for identified hazards and associated risk allows acceptance of risk with proper controls defined for the level of risk. * What in this task can cause injury or damage?
* What should be done about this situation or condition?
Assessing “likelihood” requires considering the effectiveness of hazard and risk controls, if any, currently in place. Likelihood reflects the potential and possibility for the future occurrence of a loss-producing event (A risk matrix for risk managers, 2008).

The employee contacts the supervisor and shares how the assessment was conducted, and their perception of the risk analyzed. The supervisor cannot override the concerns of the employee without providing a solution or demonstrate how the hazard and its associated risk can be controlled. Both supervisor and the employee must agree with the analysis before work can continue (refer Fig. 9.4).

The key rule is that the leadership team member cannot override the concerns of the employee without first providing a solution or demonstrating how the hazard and its associated risk are under control or can be controlled. Both the leadership team member and employee must mutually agree with the analysis before the task can be continued. The risk assessment discussion moves to the next level of leadership If:
* The supervisor and employee cannot agree on the risk rating and a solution. The supervisor cannot order the task to proceed
without the agreement with of the employee.
* A risk rating 3 has conditions or is a situation that cannot be resolved at this level of the organization.
An objective of the risk guidance card is to develop situational awareness of the potential for a loss-producing event. For a visual representation of the risk assessment, the Risk Guidance Tool uses a color code. An example of the use of color is found in the US Navy’s Operational Risk Management (ORM) Fundamentals:
* Green indicates errors may occur, but errors will be caught by the individual.
* Yellow indicates the potential for errors has increased.
* Red indicates errors may occur that cannot be caught and become consequential to the task or mission.
The target is used during team communication to focus one or more individuals into an understanding of situational conditions, or an individual can use it as a self-assessment tool to increase SA (Situational Awareness) (Operational Risk Management (ORM) Fundamentals, 2010) Because the potential for error or surprise exists in many organizational activities... information that could constitute error signals must be widely available through communication nets that can cut across departments and hierarchical levels. Communication barriers or blockages can pose a threat to feedback, evidence accumulation, and the sharing of cautionary concerns. (Boin & Schulman, 2008)

## OVERALL RISK MANAGEMENT RESPONSIBILITIES
Effective risk management specifies and defines the levels of responsibility and authority of the leadership team and may include the following:
* Defining what is an unacceptable risk and ensuring a full understanding of the potential negative impact of risk
assessed.
* Assessing risks, developing risk reduction alternatives, and defining residual risk concerns and issues.
* Identifying unnecessary or ineffective risk controls for modification or replacement.
* Selecting risk reduction options from recommendations based on risk assessment.
* Accepting or rejecting risk based on its potential severity and predetermined risk tolerance.
* Training and educating employees on the selected risk management tools and techniques. Refer Chapter 11, Developing
Safety Training Needs.
* Ensuring that risk is only taken at the appropriate organizational level, and as needed, elevating risk decisions to a higher
leadership level, that is, the organization is not exposed to a high risk based on decisions made at a low level of management. This shift implies the next higher level of leadership has a full understanding of any associated risk and its control status.
* Integrating risk assessment methods into the business planning process to ensure that time and money are budgeted for
implementation of necessary controls (Carter & Smith, 2001; Occupational Health & Safety Management Systems, 2012; Risk management toolkit, 2018).

## LEADERSHIP’S RISK RESPONSIBILITIES
Leadership’s responsibilities include:
* Consistently using risk assessment and management concepts for identifying the potential for loss-producing events.
* Ensuring employees follow appropriate risk control procedures and protocols as defined by a risk assessment, job
hazard analysis, and the safety management system.
* Quickly elevating risk concerns and issues beyond their control or authority to correct to more senior leadership for
resolution (Risk management toolkit, 2018).

## LESSON LEARNED 4
During an incident investigation session with supervisors, the initial incident investigation had a recommendation for retraining an injured employee on a standard operating procedure. A copy of the written procedure under discussion was requested as part of a job hazard analysis. It turned out the procedure did not exist. The leadership and supervision all assumed a procedure had been written, but no one had ever completed one. This lead to further discussion of the need to do the proper research on references used. It took a job hazard analysis to point out the gap and prevent an employee from being disciplined for violating a fictional SOP!

## EMPLOYEE’S RISK RESPONSIBILITIES
Employee’s responsibilities and specific roles include:
* Understanding the reasons for hazard and risk controls and ensuring controls are used, followed, and maintained.
* Maintaining a constant awareness of specific procedures, tasks, or operations and the potential severity of risk associated with them.
* Communicating immediately with their direct leadership team when risk reduction measures do not work, are unrealistic, and ineffective high-risk procedures or controls are implemented (Risk management toolkit, 2018).
* Working as a team with coworkers to ensure all appropriate hazard and risk control protocols and procedures are followed as well as assisting each other in maintaining effective controls.
* Avoiding unnecessary risks.
The use of risk assessment can improve a safety culture by strengthening and clarifying how its recommendations are presented. The focus is on the risk of inherent in an activity and not just loss information or reliance on just emphasis on regulatory compliance. An organization must meet specific production or service goals to survive, and the pressure to maintain a schedule of activity can take precedent if recommendations do not make the potential for loss as clear as possible. Employees can continue to believe the risk is acceptable even when conditions or the situation indicates otherwise.

## SUMMARY
An understanding of risk and its control must permeate an organization if an in-depth safety culture is to be sustained. A safety management system using only inspections and observations to identify hazards will not provide a full appreciation of the potential for injury and damage unless linked to the potential risk. When dealing with any hazard-related condition and its associated risk, there is a built-in affinity to judge the current environment based on past experiences. A situation starts a mental scan for memories of similar situations. Actions are then based on what consequences occurred or were experienced in the past. The selected action may not be correct or suitable for the current situation. Each time a risk is taken and nothing happens, the at-risk behavior is reinforced. A risk assessment tool can be used to develop an understanding of operational risk. The problem: many risk assessment tools used in industry restricts its use to leadership and management, and limits use by employees. A risk assessment tool can be part of a process implemented at the “grassroots level” with employees determining the potential risk associated with a perceived hazard. A risk assessment tool, in the form of a small card, can be used by employees to conduct a self-assessment before performing a task that may have inherent hazards. An underlying objective is to not add to the burden of the leadership team and employees using the tool by keeping its format as simple as possible. Using a risk assessment tool requires a shift in the perception held by leadership on what their role is as management. It requires allowing employees to raise issues without fear and with the understanding that disagreement about risk is part of the process. The process for the use of the tool includes
* Developing and implementing a simple risk matrix that is easy for all employees to understand.
* Establishing a procedure and guidelines allowing its use at any time an employee feels at risk as well as being a routine
part of tasks assessment.
* Training to ensure consistent use of all levels of the organization to assess a broad range of risks.
* Integrating it into the current safety management system.
The assessment of risk goes beyond identifying only concerns or issues that cause employee injury. Hazards by definition have the potential of causing damage to operations, harm to third parties, and even degrade the brand of the organization. The perception of risk varies from person to person. An agreed-upon baseline is needed to assess risk, define levels of risk acceptance or tolerance. A viable safety culture includes the use of risk assessment to strengthen its risk communications. Organizations continue to focus on postloss data (injuries and damage) as the primary indicator of the effectiveness of its safety program. However, injury and loss data can be influenced by organizational changes resulting in an inaccurate picture of the actual state of the safety culture and the effectiveness of safety management system. The shift from a loss-based safety management system to a focus on both loss and risk control requires a fundamental change in perception about safety. It requires letting go of long-held opinions and selective focusing on postloss events. A risk guidance card can be used to address ongoing employee perceptions or concerns about job hazards. The card can be used with a work team and employees as part of a “prejob assessment.” The card provides a “Go/No Go” decision method before performing new or assigned tasks. A safety management system should ensure risks are continuously assessed through identification and analysis of relative importance and impact. Risks are mitigated, controlled, and tracked by effectively using tools and resources such as the JHA, observation, inspections, employee participation, quality control methods such as Six Sigma, and so on. Chapter 10, Understanding Basic Risk Management Principles discusses Risk Management as an essential element of a strong safety culture. The concepts of risk management should be considered an essential part of the leadership team’s decision making. All employees need a basic understanding of the terms risk, risk control, and risk management if the organization’s safety culture is to be sustained. CHAPTER REVIEW QUESTIONS
